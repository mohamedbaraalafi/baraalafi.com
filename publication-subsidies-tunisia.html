Who Benefits from Tunisia's Subsidy System? Distributional Incidence of Fuel and Basic Commodity Subsidies

Mohamed Baraa LafiParis School of Economics (PSE)
Paper Available Soon Data & Code Available Soon

Abstract

This paper examines who benefits from Tunisia's consumer subsidy system using household-level data from the 2021 National Survey on Household Budget, Consumption and Living Standards (EBCNV), conducted by the Institut National de la Statistique (INS), together with official fiscal cost data from the Ministry of Finance's 2025 Citizen Budget. We reconstruct annual household spending on ten subsidized basic commodity and fuel products: bread and flour, semolina, couscous, pasta and rice, sugar, subsidized vegetable oil, milk, coffee, subsidized electricity and gas, bottled LPG (GPL), and transport fuels (essence and gasoil).

We find that the subsidy system is mildly regressive. The poorest 10% of the population receive 7.5% of the total subsidy value, compared with 13.8% for the richest 10%. However, the overall result hides large differences across products. Basic commodities such as semolina, couscous, pasta and rice, vegetable oil, and GPL are close to distributionally neutral or mildly pro-poor. Poor households, who represent 14.2% of the population, receive around 15–17% of the subsidy value for these products. In contrast, transport fuel subsidies are strongly regressive: poor households receive only 3.5% of the subsidy value for essence and 3.8% for gasoil, both well below their population share.

As a simple reform scenario, if the richest 40% of the population stopped receiving subsidies while the bottom 60%, including all poor households, remained fully covered, the state could save an estimated 4.71 billion DT per year, around 44% of the total fiscal cost of the products examined. We complement the decile analysis with concentration curves and bootstrap robustness checks reported in the appendix.

Overall, the results are consistent with the international evidence that universal fuel subsidies are costly and poorly targeted as a way of supporting poor households. Targeted transfers, or subsidies focused on goods that are more important for poorer households, may therefore provide better distributional results at a lower fiscal cost.
